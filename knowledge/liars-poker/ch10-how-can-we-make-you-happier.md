# Ch10 — How Can We Make You Happier?

**Source:** Michael Lewis, *Liar's Poker* (1989), Chapter 10.

## Purpose
The **Drexel/Milken threat and the Perelman takeover bid** (Sept 1987):
how the junk-bond king challenged Salomon's franchise, and the war
between the era's two bond empires — a case study in competitive rivalry
and talent economics.

## Milken's model (the contrast)
- Drexel's Beverly Hills junk-bond desk: ~85 traders, cult-like loyalty,
  extreme compensation. People were paid so much that bonuses exceeded
  Gutfreund's entire package; Milken's line: "How can we make you
  happier?"
- The system worked by paying for performance so lavishly that defection
  became irrational — loyalty bought, and bought well.
- Junk bonds were the biggest new business on Wall Street, built directly
  adjacent to Salomon's bond franchise; corporate credit quality was
  declining (Kaufman's prediction), pushing Salomon's client base toward
  Drexel.

## The feud
- 1985: Milken visited Gutfreund for breakfast; Gutfreund refused to
  treat him as an equal, they shouted, Milken was escorted out. Gutfreund
  then cut Drexel out of Salomon bond deals.
- Drexel became the center of the largest SEC investigation ever; a
  Salomon MD mailed Milken's clients the legal complaints filed against
  him.
- Sept 1987: Perelman (backed by Drexel) bid to buy a large chunk of
  Salomon — "the first time Wall Street turned and attacked its own."

## Talent economics
- At least a dozen former Salomon traders/salespeople staffed Milken's
  floor; defections were self-perpetuating (stories of magical money
  trickled back).
- Salomon's response was expulsion-style discipline (no goodbyes), which
  only accelerated the drain.
- Gutfreund's paranoia was specific: Morgan Stanley ("white shoes") he
  dismissed; Drexel — "tough like us" — he feared.

## Key takeaways
- Compensation is a strategic weapon: paying at the margin for
  performance buys loyalty and talent that retention programs can't.
- Rivalry between franchises (product, clients, talent) is the real
  market; individual trades are the visible surface.
- Personal feuds at the top (Gutfreund vs Milken) distort firm strategy —
  ego is a risk factor.
- A takeover of a people-business is a referendum on management: "our
  assets were our people… our management deserved the ax."

## Notes
- Sets up ch11's crash: the Perelman scare, the junk push, and the
  October 1987 collapse arrive together.
- The Milken compensation model prefigures modern hedge-fund pay
  structures.
