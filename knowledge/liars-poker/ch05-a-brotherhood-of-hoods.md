# Ch05 — A Brotherhood of Hoods

**Source:** Michael Lewis, *Liar's Poker* (1989), Chapter 5.

## Purpose
The birth of the **mortgage securities market** — the most important
financial innovation of the era. Covers the origin story (Bob Dall, Lew
Ranieri), the first private mortgage bond, and why mortgages became the
best job on Wall Street.

## The problem the market solved
- Until 1978, "borrowers" meant corporations and governments — not
  homeowners. Yet homeowners were the fastest-growing borrowing group,
  funded mostly through savings & loans.
- The S&L system was geographically broken: Rust Belt thrifts had
  deposits but no loan demand; Sun Belt thrifts had demand but no
  deposits. And S&Ls were funded short-term but lent long-term — a
  structural mismatch.
- 1978: Bob Dall formed Wall Street's first mortgage securities
  department at Salomon. With Stephen Joseph (Drexel's CEO's brother)
  they created the first *private* issue: Bank of America sold its home
  loans as bonds to investors (insurance companies), freeing cash to
  relend. The homeowner's payments flowed through to bondholders.
- Dall's memo (growth, aging population, Sun Belt shift) convinced
  Gutfreund to spin up a dedicated mortgage department; Ranieri was
  recruited as head trader in mid-1978.

## Ranieri's role
- Initially reluctant ("exile to Siberia") — mortgages were seen as an
  ugly stepchild: borrowers could repay at any time (prepayment
  uncertainty), and there was little to trade beyond a few Ginnie Maes.
- He was chosen for trader toughness plus "the mentality and the will to
  create a market" — and he built the research/math culture that made
  mortgages work.
- By 1984 he could plausibly argue the mortgage department made more
  money than the rest of Wall Street combined.

## Why the market exploded (next chapter's setup)
- The trigger was not Dall's megatrends — it was a 1981 **tax break for
  thrifts** (sell loans, realize losses, offset old taxes) plus the
  collapse of S&L finances after 1979's rate spike. A trillion dollars of
  home mortgages came for sale; Salomon was the only fully staffed buyer.

## Key takeaways
- Markets are created by institutions, not just discovered: a dedicated
  trader + research desk + product design (securitization) built a whole
  asset class.
- The original sin of the S&L model — borrow short, lend long — is the
  classic maturity-mismatch fragility that securitization was meant to
  (and did, briefly) fix by moving risk off balance sheets.
- Prepayment optionality is the fundamental modeling problem of
  mortgages (elaborated in ch6).

## Notes
- Directly relevant to our options knowledge: the homeowner's prepayment
  right is a call option the borrower holds — the seed of the CMO and of
  the modeling complexity that made mortgage math the era's frontier.
