# Epilogue — The End of the Game

**Source:** Michael Lewis, *Liar's Poker* (1989), Epilogue.

## Purpose
Lewis's departure in early 1988 and the book's real thesis: the money
belief — that income tracks social contribution — is false, and the
trading floor is an absurd, self-reinforcing game rather than a
meritocracy.

## The lessons he claims to take
- The most useful thing he learned was negative: **the amount of money
  one earns is not a rough guide to one's contribution to society**.
  Watching 24-year-olds (himself included) get paid out of all
  proportion to their social value refuted the belief — and once the
  belief died, so did the need to stay.
- He learned the game's skills (a "few hundred million dollars" of
  facility) but little of lasting practical value; the organization
  corrupts people, and he accepted that too.
- He left not because the firm was doomed but because he had lost the
  need to keep playing — the job had become repetition: "showing up every
  morning to do what I had already done, the reward for which was simply
  more of the same."

## Why the book matters (the meta-lesson)
- The bond-market boom was a "rare and amazing glitch" — a moment when
  unskilled young people made fortunes. The glitch was created by
  structural forces (ch3: Fed regime change, borrowing explosion) and
  institutional innovations (ch5–7: mortgages, CMOs), not by individual
  merit.
- Market participants who believe their winnings are merit are the most
  dangerous in the next cycle — they keep playing past the edge.
- The era's profit was real, but its *distribution* reflected position,
  timing, and luck more than skill — a lesson for anyone evaluating
  trading performance.

## Key takeaways
- Separating skill from luck and from structural tailwinds is the
  permanent problem of evaluating traders (and strategies) — the same
  lesson our backtesting/significance skills formalize.
- Institutional culture optimizes for continuing the game, not for truth
  about value; incentives and information are both distorted at bonus
  time.
- Knowing when to stop (not just how to play) is a form of risk
  management that the era's winners mostly lacked.

## Notes
- The epilogue closes the arc: ch1's game (Liar's Poker as market
  metaphor) is revealed as literally true — the whole decade was a
  high-stakes game whose rules favored insiders.
- For the trading repo: a durable reminder that returns attribution,
  survivorship, and structural regimes (not brilliance alone) drove the
  1980s bond profits.
