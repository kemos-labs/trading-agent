# Ch09 — The Art of War

**Source:** Michael Lewis, *Liar's Poker* (1989), Chapter 9.

## Purpose
The **German bond warrant deal** (1986): the mechanics of creating and
selling a new derivative, and the internal turf war over credit. The
chapter is a masterclass in risk transfer and the economics of
innovation.

## The trade: risk as a commodity
- Lewis's client wanted a large leveraged bet that German bonds would
  rise. Salomon was to sit in the middle and **take no risk**.
- Mechanics: create a **warrant (call option) on German bonds**.
  Risk-averse investors "sell" risk by writing the warrant; the client
  buys the risk. Salomon's profit = the difference between what it paid
  cautious investors for the risk and what it sold it to the client for
  (~$700k, risk-free to the firm).
- The insight: **risk is a commodity that can be priced, packaged, and
  resold** — buy it cheap from one investor, sell it dear to another.
  Different investors price risk differently; the middleman harvests the
  difference.
- Part of the job was "filling needs investors never knew they had" —
  creating demand for a product via the sales force, which is why a
  unique product "was pretty sure to succeed."

## Euromarket mechanics
- The Euromarket (dollar bonds issued outside the US, e.g. in London)
  fell under no government's jurisdiction — the deal needed no regulatory
  approval, only politeness (a visit to the German Finance Ministry to
  reassure them the warrant wouldn't undermine money-supply control —
  "false; the whole point was to facilitate speculation").
- First-mover advantage was the prize: being first to issue a new
  security brought publicity and franchise value beyond the fee.

## Internal competition
- An "opportunist" VP attached himself to the deal to burnish his bonus
  case, contributed the German-government diplomacy, then competed for
  credit when the deal succeeded.
- Two kinds of internal friction: fighting to pin blame when money is
  lost; fighting to claim credit when money is made. This deal was the
  latter.

## Key takeaways
- Derivatives are risk-transfer contracts: the issuer/intermediary can
  monetize the spread between investors' differing risk valuations
  without taking risk themselves.
- First-mover innovation is a franchise asset, but it's unpatentable —
  rivals replicate within 24 hours ("plagiarize").
- Internal credit-claiming is part of the game; the deal's economics and
  its internal politics are inseparable.

## Notes
- The warrant is a plain-vanilla call — the conceptual bridge between the
