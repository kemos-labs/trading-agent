# Ch01 — Liar's Poker

**Source:** Michael Lewis, *Liar's Poker* (1989), Chapter 1.

## Purpose
Opens with the legendary $1M hand of Liar's Poker between chairman John
Gutfreund and arbitrage chief John Meriwether — the book's metaphor for
what bond trading is: reading people, managing fear and greed, and
betting on probabilities plus psychology.

## The game as a trading metaphor
- Each player holds a dollar bill; the serial numbers are the "hand."
  A bid ("three sixes") claims the group's bills collectively contain ≥ 3
  sixes. You raise (higher number, or more of any number) or challenge.
  On a challenge, bills are revealed — bluffing is exposed.
- What the game tests: is this a smart risk? Am I being bluffed? Does my
  opponent know what he's doing — and if not, how do I exploit his
  ignorance? Each player looks for weakness, predictability, and pattern
  in others while avoiding it in himself.
- The math (counting probabilities of digits in random serials) is the
  *easy* part; reading faces is the hard part — the same inversion as
  markets.

## Meriwether's edge (what traders admired)
- Poker face: impossible to tell from his demeanor whether he was up or
  down — the visible control of the two emotions that destroy traders,
  **fear and greed**.
- His "boys": young Ph.D.s in math/economics/physics, trained on the
  arbitrage desk — the template for the quant desk of the 1980s.
- Gutfreund, the manager, envies traders because they *take risk*; the
  firm's money comes from risk takers, not managers.

## Key takeaways
- Markets reward reading the crowd and managing your own psychology at
  least as much as computing probabilities.
- The trader's questions are the game's questions: value, bluff
  detection, pattern exploitation.
- The episode frames the book: the era of the bond trader as a new
  aristocracy of risk.

## Notes
- The famous Gutfreund line "One hand, one million dollars, no tears"
  (1986) marks the beginning of Salomon's decline per Lewis.
- Connects to trading psychology themes echoed in Chan (risk, discipline)
  and the institutional-culture chapters that follow.
