# Ch07 — The Salomon Diet

**Source:** Michael Lewis, *Liar's Poker* (1989), Chapter 7.

## Purpose
The erosion of the mortgage monopoly: defections, narrowing spreads, and
the **CMO (collateralized mortgage obligation)** — the innovation that
completed the mortgage market but commoditized its profits.

## The monopoly dissipates
- Between 1981–85 Salomon's mortgage desk was nearly an airtight
  monopoly; by mid-1986 First Boston matched its market share.
- Mechanism of the leak: departing Salomon traders took skills, market
  understanding, AND the customer list to rivals — then had a short-term
  incentive to "educate the fool" (show customers how much Salomon was
  charging) to win their business.
- Result: margins collapsed. A trader who once bought at 94 and sold at
  95 now paid 94.5 to maybe sell at 94.55. "We started to miss trades,
  and we finally had to cut our spreads."
- Ranieri warned Gutfreund: "you're selling the technology for a hill of
  magic beans." Paying salaries rather than retention was the visible
  symptom — Salomon refused Howie Rubin's demands and replaced him with
  a dozen new Rubins who "didn't make nearly as much money."

## The CMO (invented June 1983, dominated by 1986)
- The problem: "who wants to lend money not knowing when he'll get it
  back?" Prepayment uncertainty repelled pension funds and foreign
  investors.
- CMO structure: pool hundreds of millions of mortgage bonds in a trust,
  split the certificates into **tranches** with sequential claim on
  principal prepayments:
  - Tranche 1 receives ALL principal repayments until paid off (short
    life, ~≤5 yrs).
  - Tranche 2 next (7–15 yrs).
  - Tranche 3 last (15–30 yrs).
- Effect: it *reallocated* prepayment risk across maturity buckets —
  giving investors certainty about the length of their loan. Pension
  funds (holding ~$600B in 1983, none in mortgages) put ~$30B into CMOs
  by mid-1986; $60B of CMOs were sold between 1983 and 1988.
- The irony: CMOs achieved Ranieri's goal (make mortgages look like
  ordinary bonds) — which also made them "only as profitable as other
  kinds of bonds." The innovation that built the market destroyed the
  traders' edge. Larry Fink (First Boston, co-inventor) ranks CMOs with
  junk bonds as the era's most important innovations.

## Key takeaways
- Talent and information walk out the door; customer lists are the
  franchise, and paying for retention is cheaper than rebuilding.
- Spread compression is the inevitable endgame of any profitable market
  niche once competitors learn it — monopoly edges are temporary.
- **Tranching = risk reallocation**: structuring (CMO/ABS/CDO) doesn't
  remove risk; it slices and repackages it by maturity/priority, which
  changes who holds it and what it costs. The same template returns in
  ch11's context and in later structured-credit crises.

## Notes
- CMOs prefigure the structured-credit products of the 2000s; the
  tranching logic is the direct ancestor of CDO water flows.
- For the trading repo: a case study in how innovation compresses alpha
  and how firm-level moats decay.
