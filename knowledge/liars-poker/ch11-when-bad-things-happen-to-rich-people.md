# Ch11 — When Bad Things Happen to Rich People

**Source:** Michael Lewis, *Liar's Poker* (1989), Chapter 11.

## Purpose
The **October 1987 crash and its aftermath** at Salomon: the junk-bond
push that preceded it, the eight days of firings, and the firm's near-
collapse — a case study in hubris, liquidity risk, and organizational
crisis.

## The Southland deal (the fatal leverage point)
- After the Perelman bid, Salomon jumped into junk bonds — the crowded,
  frenzied market at the top (Lewis: "a trading mistake typical of a rank
  beginner: leaping into a crowded marketplace and buying whatever is for
  sale at the top").
- Southland (7-Eleven) was bought by its own management with $4.9B of
  borrowed money; Salomon + Goldman provided the **bridge loan** that was
  supposed to be refinanced by selling junk bonds. Investors shied away —
  the bridge loan stuck on the books.
- The incentive distortion: the junk specialists who insisted Southland
  was good had the most to gain ($30M department profit) and the most to
  lose (jobs); "honesty about the quality of our merchandise was trading
  at a discount" at bonus time. Salesmen (Lewis) quietly steered
  customers away.

## The crash: eight days (Oct 12–19, 1987)
- Day 1: a board leak to the NYT that the firm would fire 1,000; two
  entire departments (municipal bonds, money markets, ~500 people)
  summarily dismissed — profitable businesses and stars cut wholesale
  because management "wasn't thinking at all but trading" (cut losses,
  move on), treating the firm as a position.
- The leak-driven panic: policy made by newspaper headline, not analysis.
- Firings skewed to the young and to women (assigned to loss leaders) —
  organizational power determined who bore the cuts.
- Oct 19 (Black Monday): the market crashed; Salomon's honoring of its
  British Petroleum underwriting commitment in the wake of the crash
  cost a $79M pre-tax loss.
- By December: $142M profit on $3.5B capital — an abysmal return — yet
  the firm broke its compensation bands to retain key producers in a
  panic trade on its own people.

## Key takeaways
- **Bridge loans / committed capital are liquidity risk**: an underwriting
  commitment you can't syndicate becomes a huge inventory position at the
  worst time.
- Entering a market late, at the top, with no informational edge is the
  classic beginner's mistake — even for an established franchise.
- Incentives corrupt information: people whose bonus depends on a deal
  can't be trusted to price it.
- Crisis management by trading instincts (cut and run, fire wholesale)
  destroys franchise value; organizational power, not merit, decides who
  absorbs losses.
- The crash exposed the culture's fragility: the giant trainee class, the
  global-expansion overhead, and the compensation arms race were all
  funded by a market that had turned.

## Notes
- The 1987 experience is the canonical warning about leverage,
  commitment risk, and hubris — themes echoed in the 1998 LTCM blowup
  (Meriwether's later fund) and 2008.
- Pairs with our Chan/Halls-Moore risk-management notes: this is what
  happens without position limits and honest valuation.
