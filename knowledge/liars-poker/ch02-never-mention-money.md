# Ch02 — Never Mention Money

**Source:** Michael Lewis, *Liar's Poker* (1989), Chapter 2.

## Purpose
How Lewis (a 24-year-old LSE economics student) got recruited to Salomon
Brothers' trading floor in 1984-85 — and the chapter's real subject: the
**social hierarchy and etiquette of the investment bank**.

## The cultural map of the bank
- **Sales & trading** (the trading floor): where money is made and risk is
  taken. Rough, competitive, meritocratic in its own brutal way. No
  secretaries, no offices, no "meetings with captains of industry."
- **Corporate finance** (investment banking proper): services the
  borrowing clients; refined, client-facing, but considered "wimps" by
  traders because they don't risk money.
- The firm's power flows to whoever produces revenue — "you point to a
  chunk of money and say, that's mine, I did that."
- Gutfreund's recruiting ethos: to succeed on the floor you must wake up
  "ready to bite the ass off a bear."

## Recruiting as social ritual
- Lewis's interview happened sideways — a managing director's wife at a
  royal charity dinner grilled him and decided he belonged on the floor.
- Wall Street recruiting valued aggression, chutzpah, and hunger over
  credentials; the firm hired by personality and sorted by performance.

## Key takeaways
- The 1980s investment bank was a status economy: traders > bankers,
  revenue > management, risk > refinement.
- Money was the scoreboard but never to be discussed directly — the
  etiquette ("never mention money") was part of the culture's signal of
  seriousness.
- The entry story shows how much of finance hiring was social matching,
  not skill assessment — relevant context for how institutional culture
  perpetuates itself.
