# Ch06 — The Fat Men and Their Marvelous Money Machine

**Source:** Michael Lewis, *Liar's Poker* (1989), Chapter 6.

## Purpose
The core market-mechanics chapter: how the mortgage machine made money —
the S&L crisis, whole-loan trading, **prepayment modeling**, and the
structural advantage of knowing the value the seller didn't.

## The supply shock (1981)
- Fed rate spike (1979) bled thrifts: paying 14% on deposits while taking
  in ~5% on old loans. Congress's Sept 30, 1981 tax break let thrifts sell
  loans at a loss (65¢ on the dollar), amortize the loss over the loan's
  life, and offset taxes from the prior 10 years — making "generate
  losses" the rational strategy. A trillion dollars of mortgages flooded
  the market; Salomon had the only fully staffed mortgage desk — a
  government-created monopoly.

## The toll-taker's dream
- Desperate sellers (thrift presidents) cared *when* they were paid more
  than *how much*. Traders bought at 75, sold near-identical loans at 85
  — a $10M "fee" on a $100M swap — and the customers came back for more
  ("if you don't do it, you're out of a job").
- Knowledge asymmetry: thrifts often didn't know the value, terms, or
  maturity of their own loans. Salomon priced bonds properly; the
  customer didn't know who the fool was.
- Even Ranieri occasionally forced a trader to unwind a rip-off (the
  80 → 83 → 80.25 repricing story) to protect the customer franchise.

## Whole loans and prepayment
- Salomon graduated from trading mortgage *bonds* to buying the raw
  material — **whole loans** (home loans), effectively becoming a thrift,
  exposed to homeowner default. Ranieri traded them anyway (99.9% of the
  market) and got FHA approval ("the eagle") after the fact.
- The key modeling problem: **prepayment** — homeowners repay early.
  Howie Rubin built statistical prepayment models from Salomon's research
  data: probability of prepayment varied by location, loan age, and loan
  size. Buying a bond at 60 that prepays at 100 is the windfall.
- Two prepayment-prone loan classes: financially distressed properties
  (government-guaranteed, pay in full on default) and upmarket properties
  likely to be converted (paid off by the new owner). Roth/Brittenham
  made $10M roaming housing projects for pools and bankrupt tenants.

## The culture
- The mortgage desk became loud, rude, and phenomenally profitable
  (research → math → money). Monthly department dinners, Atlantic City
  trips, goofs — a tribe united against the rest of the firm.

## Key takeaways
- Policy (tax/accounting rules) can create or destroy whole markets; the
  trader's job is to be on the right side of the resulting flow.
- Information asymmetry is the spread: knowing the value the seller
  doesn't know is the toll-taker's whole edge.
- Prepayment is an embedded option; modeling it (by cohort, geography,
  incentive) was the first systematic mortgage analytics — the ancestor
  of today's MBS prepayment models.

## Notes
- The "buy at 60, paid off at 100" dynamic is optionality payoff: the
  bondholder is short the homeowner's call (prepayment) but long the
  risk of holding a par bond — complexity the CMO (ch7) tried to manage.
