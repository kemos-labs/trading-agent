# Ch15 — Conclusion

**Source:** *Volatility Trading*, 2nd ed. — Euan Sinclair (Wiley, 2013)

## The central theme

Successful trading is about developing a **consistent process**: (1) define
one clear goal; (2) find trades with edge; (3) capture that edge via
hedging; (4) size each trade consistently with the goal. Everything else is
done within this framework.

- **A goal must be one thing**: "as much money as possible with minimal
  risk and steady income" is three goals. State it in one sentence or you
  don't have one. Add goals only after achieving the first.
- **Trading is a serious business**: there are no minor leagues; we learn
  while playing in the majors. Obsess over what you can control (process,
  records, costs, discipline) because much is uncontrollable.

## What we must understand to trade vol

- **Volatility**: its clustering and mean reversion are the source of most
  of its predictability; measure it well (range estimators), forecast it
  over the trade's horizon, and respect its fat tails.
- **Option pricing**: options are the tool (the "gun") for trading
  volatility (the "prey"). You don't need stochastic calculus, but you must
  understand the model's assumptions and how well they apply to real
  markets.
- **Edge**: any successful methodology has a clear statistical edge. The
  most direct route is measuring/forecasting realized vol and trading its
  divergence from implied — while being able to explain *why* the market
  has it wrong (news, sector trends, behavioral psychology). Without a
  known edge, don't trade.
- **Hedging**: converts option prices into volatility trades; it makes the
  business path-dependent — you can be right and still lose. That
  dissonance makes vol trading inherently statistical.
- **Sizing**: evaluate each trade by projected return and risk in the
  context of the goal; a good sizing scheme turns a string of highly
  uncertain trades into a consistently profitable business.
- **Records**: comprehensive, accurate P/L records are mandatory — without
  them there is no way to improve.

## Beyond knowledge

Knowledge is necessary but not sufficient; all else equal the trader with
more knowledge does better, but all else is rarely equal:

- **Execution ability** — often the single most important skill; the best
  idea poorly executed loses to a worse idea well executed.
- **Concentration** — showing up and working hard all day beats brilliance
  that wanders.
- **Product selection** — a huge factor: the best trader in a dead product
  struggles; mediocre traders make money in busy products. Choose your
  battlefield deliberately.
- **The mundane** — reliable software/hardware, a positive attitude, a
  consistent routine, enough sleep.

## Final framing

- A **model** is not a **strategy**, and a strategy is not a **business**.
- Markets always change → always be learning; knowledge itself is a source
  of edge.
- The aim is to make money, not to prove how good a trader you are.

## Key takeaways

- Process over prediction: one goal, explicit edge, disciplined capture,
  consistent sizing.
- Technical knowledge is learnable and necessary but insufficient —
  execution, concentration, product selection, and records complete it.
- Measure, forecast, and respect volatility; hedge to convert; size to
  survive; record to improve.
