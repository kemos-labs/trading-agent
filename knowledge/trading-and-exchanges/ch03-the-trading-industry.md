# Ch03 — The Trading Industry (Brokers, Dealers, and Their Clients)

**Source:** Larry Harris, *Trading and Exchanges* (2003), Chapter 3.

## Purpose
Details the sell-side: who brokers and dealers are, how they make
money, and the agency conflicts built into the broker–client
relationship.

## Brokers
- Act as **agents** for clients; their obligations are to seek the best
  available execution (price, speed, certainty) and to avoid trading
  ahead of or against client orders.
- Make money via **commissions** and via **payments for order flow**
  (rebates for routing orders to specific dealers).
- Agency problem: brokers' interests (maximize order flow, collect
  rebates) can diverge from clients' interests (best execution).
  Regulation (SEC rules, best-execution duties) exists because clients
  cannot easily audit execution quality.

## Dealers
- Trade as **principals**: buy at the bid, sell at the ask, profiting
  from the spread and from favorable inventory movement.
- Provide liquidity by standing ready to trade when no counterparty is
  present — they are compensated for the **adverse selection** risk of
  trading with informed traders and for their **inventory risk**.
- Make money three ways: spread capture, price appreciation of
  inventory, and (in some markets) payments for order flow from
  brokers.

## Key structural facts
- Dealers compete to obtain order flow; order flow is valuable because
  it gives them a chance to profit and (crucially) because uninformed
  order flow is *profitable* to trade against.
- **Preferencing** — brokers routing orders to particular dealers in
  exchange for payments — is widespread; whether it harms clients
  depends on execution quality vs. the primary market's.
- **Dual trading** (dealer also brokering) creates conflicts that
  exchanges mitigate with precedence rules and prohibitions on trading
  ahead.

## Key takeaways
- The broker–client relationship is a textbook principal–agent
  problem; the industry's regulatory architecture (best execution, Rule
  11Ac1-5/6 disclosure) is designed around the fact that clients cannot
  measure execution quality themselves.
- Dealers are not parasites: they absorb order flow imbalances and
  carry inventory, services for which the spread is the price.
- When designing or choosing a trading venue, price the **total
  execution cost** (spread + commissions + price impact), not just the
  visible commission.
