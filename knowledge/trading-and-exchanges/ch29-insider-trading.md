# Ch29 — Insider Trading

**Source:** Larry Harris, *Trading and Exchanges* (2003), Chapter 29.

## Purpose
What insider trading is, how it is detected and prosecuted, and the
economic debate over whether restricting it helps or hurts markets.

## The law
- **Definition** (SEC): trading on material nonpublic information in
  breach of a fiduciary duty or trust relationship; includes tipping,
  trading by tippees, and **misappropriation** (stealing information
  even without a fiduciary duty to the issuer — the Chiarella case:
  a printer who deduced takeover targets was initially acquitted, but
  the doctrine later covered him).
- Material information stays "inside" until it reaches the public;
  Regulation FD (2000) requires issuers to disclose material
  information to everyone at once (killing the analyst-preview
  privilege).
- Insiders must report trades (initial purchases within 10 days);
  **Rule 10b5-1** prearranged plans let insiders trade without
  prosecution.

## Detection
- Exchange surveillance: unusual price/volume moves before news
  releases; investigators build two lists (who knew the information,
  who profited from the trades) and find the links — the "degrees of
  separation" network search (Colt Industries: 40 friends/relatives
  bought deep OTM options after a law-firm colleague leaked).
- Profitable patterns: deep out-of-the-money options are the favored
  insider vehicle (max leverage, low capital).

## The debate
**For restriction**:
- **Fairness**: markets perceived as fair attract investors →
  higher prices → lower cost of capital.
- **Liquidity**: insiders are informed traders; removing them lowers
  adverse selection, narrowing spreads and cutting uninformed
  transaction costs (the zero-sum logic: informed profits = uninformed
  losses).
- **Corporate control**: insiders shouldn't profit from the firm's
  own information at shareholders' expense.

**Against restriction**:
- **Slower price discovery**: insider trading reveals information
  through prices; restricting it slows adjustment (though competition
  among insiders reveals it fast — collusion to trade slowly is what
  restriction actually *encourages*).
- **Compensation**: where insiders can trade, cash compensation is
  lower (a substitute); restriction raises direct pay.
- Markets can also be seen as more volatile without the information
  channel.

## Key takeaways
- Insider trading is an **adverse-selection subsidy** problem: it
  taxes uninformed liquidity providers, and the restriction debate is
  really about who should own information rents.
- The most informed flow is invisible: surveillance relies on
  *abnormal patterns* — which is exactly why unusual options volume
  before news is a red flag in any market.
- For practitioners: the legal boundary is "material + nonpublic +
  duty"; trading on your own analysis of public data is the safe and
  legitimate lane.
