# Ch26 — Competition Within and Among Markets

**Source:** Larry Harris, *Trading and Exchanges* (2003), Chapter 26.

## Purpose
Why markets consolidate (the order flow externality), why
fragmentation persists, and the forces that keep fragmented markets
coherent.

## The order flow externality
- Traders gravitate to the most liquid market; each new trader adds
  liquidity, attracting more traders — a **network effect** that
  consolidates markets without regulation.
- Consequence: an incumbent market has a huge advantage. A new venue
  can fail even if superior (the **Optimark** case: $406M lost) simply
  because no one wants to be the first to switch.
- ECNs beat Nasdaq (but not the NYSE) because they integrated with
  Nasdaq's routing — orders stayed exposed to the incumbent's
  liquidity — and because their precedence rules allowed them to
  take priority over Nasdaq quotes.

## Why fragmentation persists
- Traders differ: impatient traders want dealer certainty (quote-
  driven markets), patient traders want low cost and time priority
  (order-driven). No one structure serves both.
- **Unequal access** (members vs. off-floor), **unequal
  creditworthiness** (some traders must be excluded), and **preference
  heterogeneity** (some want anonymity, some want immediacy) all
  create niches for alternative venues.
- **Preferencing** can pull order flow away from the primary market,
  which then becomes less attractive to limit order traders.

## How fragmented markets consolidate
1. **Information flows** between fragments (public quotes/trades).
2. **Order routing**: liquidity demanders send orders to the most
   liquid segment; suppliers to the hungriest.
3. **Arbitrageurs** move liquidity between segments, enforcing the
   law of one price.
- With free information and routing, a fragmented market behaves as
  one unified complex — fragmentation costs are bounded by the cost of
  the information/routing infrastructure plus arbitrage capital.

## Key takeaways
- Liquidity is a **winner-take-most network good**: the first mover
  with the most flow wins, regardless of technical merit.
- Fragmentation is not bad per se — it serves heterogeneous traders —
  but it requires public information and arbitrage to stay
  consolidated in price.
- When choosing venues, the externality is your friend (trade where
  everyone trades) and your enemy (the incumbent can extract rents);
  routing costs and information quality decide the balance.
