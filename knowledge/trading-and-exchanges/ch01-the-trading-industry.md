# Ch01 — The Trading Industry

**Source:** Larry Harris, *Trading and Exchanges* (2003), Chapter 1.

## Purpose
Defines the trading industry and why the book's microstructure lens
matters: the industry's core economic function is to help traders
arrange mutually beneficial trades, and every market institution is a
response to the costs of finding counterparties.

## What "trading" means
- The trading industry transfers ownership of securities and provides
  liquidity. Its fundamental product is **transactional arrangements**
  — helping buyers and sellers find each other and trade.
- Two broad classes of participants: **buy-side traders** (investors,
  speculators, hedgers) who trade because they want positions, and
  **sell-side traders** (brokers, dealers, market makers) who help the
  buy side trade and profit from serving it.

## Why people trade (the taxonomy)
1. **Investors** — shift wealth from the present to the future
   (deferring consumption).
2. **Traders** — exchange risk (hedgers), speculate on information
   (informed traders), or act on mispricing (value traders / arbitrageurs).
3. **Utilitarian traders** — solve non-information problems: asset
   exchangers (swap one security for another), borrowers/lenders
   (move money through time), hedgers (transfer risk).
4. **Frivolous traders** — gamblers and order anticipators who trade for
   entertainment or to exploit others.

## The industry's roles
- **Brokers** act as agents for buyers and sellers (may or may not
  trade against them); **dealers** trade as principals, buying and
  selling from their own inventory.
- **Exchanges** provide rules and facilities for trading; **OTC
  markets** (over the counter) are negotiated, dealer-based.
- Markets exist because of **transaction costs**: search costs (finding
  a counterparty), information costs, and the bid/ask spread that
  compensates liquidity suppliers for the risk of trading with better-
  informed people.

## Key takeaways
- Every feature of market structure — order types, trading floors,
  dealers, limit order books — exists to solve a specific transactional
  problem for a specific trader type.
- The fundamental tension: markets that serve impatient traders
  (immediate execution, dealer-quoted) differ from markets that serve
  patient traders (low cost, order-driven) — and no single design
  serves both perfectly.
- Trading is not a zero-sum game in the service dimension: better
  transactional arrangements make the whole economy wealthier by
  lowering the cost of allocating capital and risk.
