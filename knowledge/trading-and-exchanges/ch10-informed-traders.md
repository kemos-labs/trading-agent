# Ch10 — Informed Traders

**Source:** Larry Harris, *Trading and Exchanges* (2003), Chapter 10.

## Purpose
How informed traders behave and why their presence is simultaneously
the market's best feature (price discovery) and its biggest cost
(adverse selection).

## Who they are and how they trade
- Informed traders hold private information about value: insiders,
  news traders with faster access, analysts, and traders who can
  process public data first.
- To profit, they must trade **before** information becomes public —
  hence their need for **speed and anonymity**.
- They demand liquidity: market orders (and marketable limits) execute
  now, before the information leaks; limit orders expose them to being
  picked off as the price moves through.

## Adverse selection
- When an informed trader takes a market order, the counterparty (limit
  order trader or dealer) is *systematically* on the wrong side.
- Liquidity suppliers cannot distinguish informed from uninformed
  flow, so they widen spreads to all traders to cover expected losses
  to informed traders (see Ch14 for the spread decomposition).
- The **winner's curse** of limit orders: your order executes
  preferentially when you are wrong (prices moving against you), so
  the fill itself is bad news.

## Why informed trading is good
- Their trading moves prices toward fundamental value — this is
  **price discovery**, the mechanism that makes market prices
  informative and allocates capital efficiently.
- Their expected profits are the *research incentive*: without the
  chance to profit from private information, nobody would invest in
  generating it, and prices would become stale.

## Key takeaways
- Informed traders are the market's information channel; the spread is
  the fee everyone else pays to fund their research.
- The strategic problem for an informed trader is **execution**
  (how to trade without revealing too much) — the same problem an
  institutional order faces; splitting, timing, and anonymity are the
  levers.
- For liquidity suppliers: the probability of informed flow is the
  central input to pricing; raising it widens spreads and thins depth
  in a predictable way.
