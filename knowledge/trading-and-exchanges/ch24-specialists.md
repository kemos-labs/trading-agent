# Ch24 — Specialists

**Source:** Larry Harris, *Trading and Exchanges* (2003), Chapter 24.

## Purpose
The designated-primary-market-maker system: specialists' obligations,
privileges, and the economics of regulated liquidity provision.

## The three roles
1. **Dealer** — trade for their own account (the regulated part).
2. **Broker** — represent orders left by floor brokers (earning
   commissions).
3. **Exchange official** — conduct an orderly market, set the opening
   price, and maintain continuity.

## Obligations
- **Affirmative**: always quote a meaningful two-sided market when no
  one else will (liquidity of last resort); smooth prices to create
  **price continuity** (prevent large reversals).
- **Negative**: yield to public orders — the **public order
  precedence rule** (can't trade at a price where public orders
  stand), the **price priority rule**, and the **public liquidity
  preservation principle** (can't fill standing limit orders for
  their own account ahead of public traders).

## Privileges
- Monopoly over the stock: order flow, the book, and floor
  information; the right to **stop orders** (guarantee a price,
  delaying execution — a free option for the specialist, costly to
  limit order traders); setting the **opening price** after seeing all
  orders (the "opening option"); brokerage commissions; and dealer
  profits from supplying liquidity to marketable flow.
- The monopoly is contestable in principle but not in practice: the
  **order flow externality** (specialists who get flow learn the most
  and serve best, attracting more flow) entrenches incumbents.

## Economics and regulation
- Specialists fund their volatility losses with normal-time profits;
  this works only while their exchange has >50% market share (else
  beneficiaries free-ride). Exchanges therefore demand continuity
  only when they can capture the benefits.
- **Capital adequacy standards** ensure specialists can fund
  extraordinary-market liquidity; performance is evaluated on spread
  width, depth, reversal frequency/size, and price-improvement rates.
- The system transfers wealth from limit order traders (benefactors)
  to small uninformed market order traders (beneficiaries).

## Key takeaways
- Obligation-based liquidity is **fragile by design**: it depends on a
  profitable monopoly whose value is eroded by competition and
  fragmentation.
- The specialist's edge is *information and discretion*: seeing the
  whole book and choosing the clearing price after others commit.
- In modern markets, the same tensions live on in designated market
  makers and in obligations attached to maker rebates — the design
  question (who must provide liquidity, and how are they paid?) is
  unchanged.
