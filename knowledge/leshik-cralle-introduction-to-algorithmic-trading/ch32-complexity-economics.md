# Chapter 32 — Complexity Economics

Source: Leshik & Cralle, *An Introduction to Algorithmic Trading* (2011)

## Complexity vs. traditional economics

- No widely accepted definition of "complexity" in economics yet — the
  field is "under construction". Santa Fe Institute thinkers (Brian
  Arthur, Kenneth Arrow, Durlauf, Lane, Kauffman, Farmer, Packard).
- **Traditional economics** models were analogies to early thermodynamics
  — First Law (equilibrium, no free lunch, matter conserved). Carnot
  (1824) and Clausius (1860) formalized the Second Law: entropy grows
  in any spontaneous process (heat flows warm→cold). Traditional
  economics "has not adapted to the Second Law" — incomplete at the
  roots. It also ignores **information entropy** (Shannon & Weaver,
  1949, based on Boltzmann's statistical thermodynamics) — the
  "information uncertainty" associated with a probability distribution.
- Traditional models use few degrees of freedom (simplifying
  assumptions) — e.g., the unemployment/inflation relation as a
  one-degree-of-freedom function. Conceived before computers.

## The Santa Fe complexity view (paraphrased)

- **Continual adaptation**: behaviors, actions, strategies, products
  are revised as agents accumulate experience — the system
  (participants + rules + physical computing/connectivity layer)
  constantly adapts.
- **Perpetual novelty niches**: new markets, technologies, behaviors,
  institutions create new niches; filling a niche creates more —
  ongoing novelty. Agents seek trading advantage; new agents enter,
  others retreat.
- **Out-of-equilibrium dynamics**: the economy operates far from any
  optimum/global equilibrium; improvements are always possible, occur
  regularly/continuously, and are the natural state.
- **Entropy** has been used since ~1988 to describe
  organization/disorder as basic state parameters in complex
  (including economic) system evolution.

## Synthesis

Complexity economics draws on behavioral, institutional, and
evolutionary economics + statistical mechanics. "Wall Street suffers
from physics envy" — add biology envy + multidimensional math (still
out of reach computationally) + human frailties (greed, envy, fear).
Complex systems include emergence, sensitive dependence on initial
conditions, and selection mechanisms (evolutionary models).

## Key takeaways

1. Markets are evolutionary, out-of-equilibrium systems — don't model
   them as equilibrium-seeking thermodynamic analogues.
2. Information entropy and multi-dimensional adaptation are missing
   from mainstream models — computational simulation now lets macro
   behavior emerge from micro rules without idealizing participants.
3. Expect perpetual novelty: niches fill and create new niches;
   algorithmic strategies must continually adapt.
