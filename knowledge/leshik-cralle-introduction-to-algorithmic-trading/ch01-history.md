# Chapter 1 — History

Source: Leshik & Cralle, *An Introduction to Algorithmic Trading* (2011)

## Context

- "Algorithm" derives from Al-Khwarizmi (c. 820 AD, Persian/Uzbek
  mathematician, *Treatise on the Calculation with Arabic Numerals*);
  roots of "algebra" (al-jabr). Earliest formal algorithm: Euclid's GCD
  (c. 300 BC).
- The book's scope: algorithms applied to **stock trading strategies**
  — Part I surveys the Tier-1 (institutional) algo landscape; Part II is
  the authors' own practical method for individual traders (US equity,
  NASDAQ/NYSE).

## Lineage of program trading

- 1949: Alfred Winslow Jones founds the first hedge fund — balanced
  long/short with ≈30:70 short-to-long ratio.
- 1960s: early computer enthusiasts analyze stock price movement with
  mainframes; Peter N. Haurlan (rocket scientist at JPL) one of the
  first to compute exponential moving averages on stock data
  (Trade Levels Reports).
- 1970s: block trading goes mainstream (block = $1M+ value or 10,000+
  shares) — contentious at the time.
- c. 1980: **pair trading / statistical arbitrage (statarb)** invented
  at Morgan Stanley by Nunzio Tartaglia's multidisciplinary team led by
  Gerald Bamberger; hugely profitable, a Wall Street cult. Spawned the
  pioneers (David Shaw, James Simons et al.) — "the Black Box was born."
- Exponential compute growth (Moore's Law) + **Direct Market Access
  (DMA)** for non-exchange members let individual traders run algos by
  themselves. Buy-side and sell-side recruited scientists ("quants" —
  physics, statistics, math) from Stanford/Harvard/MIT, shifting the
  culture away from the "high personality" floor trader.

## Key takeaways

1. Algo trading's modern roots are statarb (1980s Morgan Stanley) and
   DMA; the lineage runs through Renaissance/DE Shaw-style quants.
2. The intended audience here is the *individual trader* in US equities
   — a practical, Excel-centric method rather than institutional HFT.
