# Ch21 — Very Dangerous

**Source:** Scott Patterson, *Dark Pools* (2012), Chapter 21.

## Purpose
The political and regulatory aftermath of the Flash Crash: senators
demand answers, exchanges blame each other, the SEC imposes circuit
breakers, and the agency's joint report admits it cannot explain what
happened — while the market keeps fragmenting into darkness.

## The Senate moment
- Senator Mark Warner: "We may have seen the first inkling today with
  what happens when these tools of technology do not work the way they
  are supposed to." Ted Kaufman: "We basically went from a market that
  was floor-based to a market that was digitalized and decimalized...
  No one knows what is happening in these exchanges... We have a very
  dangerous situation."
- May 7: Nasdaq CEO Greifeld attacks the NYSE for halting stocks ("they
  basically walked away"); NYSE CEO Niederauer fires back, then admits
  the arms race "is not sustainable" — the chief architect of electronic
  trading denouncing runaway electronic trading.

## The regulatory response
- SEC chair Mary Schapiro summons the exchange chiefs (Niederauer,
  Greifeld, BATS's Ratterman, Direct Edge's O'Brien) and orders
  **individual-stock circuit breakers** — a reversal of the speed-first
  regime.
- The SEC/CFTC joint report (May 18, 2010, just 12 days later) finds a
  "mismatch in liquidity" worsened by "the withdrawal of liquidity by
  electronic market makers" — and admits the trigger is unknown:
  "We have found no evidence that these events were triggered by 'fat
  finger' errors, computer hacking, or terrorist activity."

## The market's response: more darkness
- Retail cash flees stock funds for the rest of 2010 even as the market
  rebounds — a crisis of faith.
- Glitches keep happening: Infinium Capital (Aug 2010) machine-guns
  2–3k orders/second into oil, popping prices $1; Washington Post
  tumbles; Progress Energy plunges ~90% in seconds.
- Trading spreads across ~70 venues; dark pools and internalizers reach
  ~40% of U.S. volume (Tabb Group), up from 15% in 2008.
- The book's thesis crystallizes: the architects (Levine, Putnam)
  believed they were bringing light; the computerization instead made
  secret formulas and plumbing knowledge the "currency of the realm" —
  the market became a high-tech poker game of programmer vs. programmer,
  algo vs. algo.

## Key takeaways
- Post-crash, the dominant regulatory instinct is to slow things down —
  circuit breakers are an admission that speed was over-valued.
- The market's growing opacity (70 venues, 40% dark) is itself a risk:
  nobody — not even the SEC — can see the whole picture.
- Narrative framing (who "walked away") matters as much as the
  mechanics in the political battle.
