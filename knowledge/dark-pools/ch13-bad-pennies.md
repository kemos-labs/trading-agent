# Ch13 — Bad Pennies

**Source:** Scott Patterson, *Dark Pools* (2012), Chapter 13.

## Purpose
Goldman Sachs backs Archipelago, and Island single-handedly forces the
market's shift to **decimalization** — the regulatory change that
compressed spreads to pennies, drove human market makers out of
business, and opened the door for high-frequency traders.

## Goldman's Trojan horse
- Summer 1999: Goldman's John Hewitt ("Spock") and Duncan Niederauer
  invest $25M in Archipelago, then reveal the real plan at a board
  meeting in the Sears Tower: "We're going to be the electronic trading
  arm of the New York Stock Exchange." Goldman had bought Hull Trading
  for $500M and was wiring itself into every corner of electronic
  trading. *Liquidity breeds liquidity* becomes the mantra.
- Putnam, who hates the NYSE's specialist monopoly ("You're a
  monopoly!"), launches stunts: an armored car handing out pennies
  outside the Big Board, "DON'T GET PENNIED" signs — attacking the
  front-running practice of specialists buying cheap and selling
  pennies higher.

## Decimalization
- Nasdaq and NYSE quoted stocks in fractions (min step 1/16 = 6.25¢),
  keeping spreads wide. Regulators/congress push pennies; the NYSE and
  Nasdaq stall. Oxley's "Common Cents Stock Pricing Act" (1997) forces a
  pledge to decimalize by 2000, then the SEC mandates July 3, 2000 —
  delayed under Nasdaq pressure.
- Levine had believed in penny (and even sub-penny) quotes since the
  1980s. Island simply **goes decimal on its own**: the first penny
  trade — Congressman Tom Bliley buys Nextel — is staged in Washington;
  within months >10% of Island orders are decimal. The pressure works:
  the whole market decimalizes within a year.
- Spreads collapse: Intel's bid/offer races from $20.10/$20.20 down to
  $20.15/$20.16 — one penny. Hyper-progressive quoting is impossible for
  humans, trivial for computers.
- Levine later proposes sub-penny trading (4 decimal places, e.g.,
  $20.0099) — the SEC refuses (sub-penny restriction still mostly
  stands); he likens the ban to forcing car dealers to sell in $1,000
  increments.

## Consequences
- Human market makers and NYSE specialists drop out in droves as
  spreads compress; high-frequency traders take their place. The
  decimalization the incumbents feared becomes their exit.

## Key takeaways
- Tick size is a structural subsidy to intermediaries; shrinking it
  transfers rent from market makers to fast computers.
- A single venue can force an industry-wide standard by acting alone
  (Island's unilateral decimal quoting) — credible unilateral action
  accelerates regulation.
- Penny spreads + speed = machines replace humans at the core of
  market making.
