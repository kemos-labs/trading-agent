# Ch02 — The Size Game

**Source:** Scott Patterson, *Dark Pools* (2012), Chapter 2.

## Purpose
Bodek's origin story — and a portrait of the pre-electronic market
structure (NYSE specialists vs. Nasdaq market makers) whose "size game"
later motivated the SOES bandits and the rise of electronic pools.

## Bodek's formation
- Son of Arie Bodek, a particle physicist whose work helped establish
  the existence of the quark but who never won the Nobel — a family
  wound that drove Haim's obsession with *predicting the future*.
- As a teen he rebelled (thrash-metal drummer), then channeled the
  drive into AI: math + cognitive science at Rochester, then Magnify in
  Oak Park, Illinois, where he helped build a machine-learning
  **credit-card-fraud detector** (gas-station test purchase → big
  jewelry splurge pattern) that Visa adopted — scanning 300,000
  transactions/hour. The template: find order in massive data.

## Hull Trading and the old market
- In 1997 Bodek joins **Hull Trading** (founded 1985 by mathematician,
  trader, and blackjack whiz Blair Hull), an elite options house full
  of physicists from Fermilab. His first assignment: machine learning
  to predict option-market direction.
- The 1997 market: two venues — the NYSE floor with specialists and
  hand signals, and Nasdaq with ~500 phone-based market makers. Humans
  were everywhere; the market was too clunky for adaptive algos. Bodek
  shifts to European options, which are more electronic.
- 1999: Goldman buys Hull for ~$500M — a watershed that pivots Goldman
  toward electronic trading. Bodek stays as an insider-outsider.

## The "size game"
- The chapter's title refers to the market-maker practice of quoting
  **size** (share quantities) that they had no intention of honoring at
  that price, and to the game of who controls displayed size. This is
  the exploitable flaw the SOES bandits weaponize in later chapters:
  quotes were promises the dealers could often dodge.

## Key takeaways
- Machine learning on massive behavioral data (fraud detection) is the
  same toolkit later aimed at markets.
- Market structure — not just strategy — decides who can win; the old
  human-mediated structure throttled adaptive algorithms.
- Mergers (Hull→Goldman) transplant elite quant culture into the
  establishment, accelerating the Algo Wars.
