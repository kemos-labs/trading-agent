# Ch12 — Palace Coup

**Source:** Scott Patterson, *Dark Pools* (2012), Chapter 12.

## Purpose
The corporate reckoning of Datek/Island's founders: Ed Nicoll's palace
coup against Jeff Citron (October 6, 1999) as legal scrutiny mounts,
Island's explosive dot-com-era growth, and its first major hardware
failure in the March 2000 crash.

## The coup
- July 1999: the Justice Department subpoenas Datek Online's records.
  Nicoll (ex-TD Waterhouse cofounder, brought in as president/COO)
  declares the investigation a "materially adverse event," letting
  investors out: Vulcan pulls, TA/Groupe Arnault cut to $195M.
- Nicoll decides Citron must go — a liability who could sink the firm.
  On the morning of Oct 6, 1999 he sends Citron two press releases and
  lets him choose: (1) "CEO Jeffrey Citron is resigning," or (2) "the
  entire upper management except Citron is stepping down." Citron, 29,
  picks the first — owner of 30% of a >$1B paper company. (He later
  co-founds Vonage.)
- Maschler and Citron cash out of Datek Online: ~$700M for their 52%.

## Island's scale
- Sept 1999: Island introduces a 12-hour trading day (8am–8pm ET) —
  electronic trading doesn't need the NYSE's 6.5-hour day; Datek Online
  becomes the first online broker with extended hours.
- Volume: 17M shares/day (late 1997) → 90M (Q1 1999); 27B shares in
  all of 1999 ($1.6T); Apr 4, 2000 alone: 126.8M shares ($18.3B).
- Dec 2000: Island spun off from Datek Online as a separate company;
  Nicoll chairman, Andresen CEO, Levine — again — no title.
- By early 2000, ~15% of all Nasdaq stocks flow through Island's pipes.

## The March 2000 crash
- Nasdaq peaks at 5,049 on Friday Mar 10, 2000; on Monday a pre-open
  sell wave through Island (a haven for before-hours trading) precedes a
  ~170-point open gap; within days Nasdaq craters ~10%.
- Mar 24, 2000: Island suffers a catastrophic hardware failure (half its
  backup computers out), a broken AC system (temperatures >100°F, an
  18-wheeler air-conditioning rig parked outside) — yet keeps trading
  smoothly. Users (Andy Madoff's email) note that REDI, ARCA, and B-Trade
  all had problems while Island held up: reliability becomes Island's
  brand.

## Key takeaways
- Legal/regulatory risk is existential for market infrastructure
  firms — reputational contagion forced founder exits regardless of
  profitability.
- Pre-market/after-hours liquidity concentrates in the fastest venue —
  a structural advantage that also transmits overnight shocks.
- Operational resilience (fault-tolerant design) is a durable
  competitive moat.
