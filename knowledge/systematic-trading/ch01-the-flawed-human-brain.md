# Ch01 — The Flawed Human Brain

**Source:** Carver, *Systematic Trading*, Chapter 1.

## Why humans fail as traders
Humans have an astonishing ability to absorb complex market data, yet are
"deeply flawed when making financial decisions." Carver's own BP trades show
both failure modes: selling winners too early (2004, small profit, stock then
rallied hard) and holding losers too long (2010, held from £6 to the £3 bottom).
Cognitive biases overwhelm any natural advantage over simple decision rules.

## Behavioural finance basics
- Classical finance (1950s) assumed **rational economic man** and efficient
  markets; anomalies were dismissed. Psychology researchers from the 1980s
  brought **behavioural finance** and its unifying model, **prospect theory**:
  our brains are still hardwired for survival, favouring quick over thoughtful
  decisions.
- **Why we run losses & stop out profits**: people are reluctant to realise
  losses ("get-evenitis", Shefrin) — a paper loss isn't felt as real until
  crystallised, and selling admits a mistake. Winners are sold fast to
  minimise regret and confirm the original buy was right. Prospect theory:
  more risk taken in losing positions, less when winning.
- Carver back-tested an "early profit taker" vs an "early loss taker" on 31
  futures: early loss taker won in 27. The rule that mimics instinct does
  worse — and it earns extra returns exploiting others' persistence.
- **Addiction parallels**: addictive games have (1) illusion of control, (2)
  apparent near-misses, (3) rapid/continuous play. Active investing has the
  illusion of control; day trading adds the other two — churning trades
  resembles slot-machine behaviour.

## The solution: simple, objective trading rules
- Systematic rules mitigate our flaws *and* exploit other humans' biases
  (behavioural anomalies persist because of cognitive bias).
- **Sticking to the plan / meddling**: overconfidence — "we think we are
  cleverer than the trading system and we are" — drives *meddling*. Need a
  **commitment mechanism** (Odysseus tying himself to the mast; Niederhoffer's
  Susan enforcing his pre-committed loss limit).
- **Systems must be objective**: subjective rules ("sell for small losses")
  get redefined under stress. Objective rules create "a line in the sand" and
  are the only ones that can be back-tested.
- **Automation** is a commitment mechanism ("computer, a man and a dog" joke:
  the dog bites the man if he touches the computer), but only if you *trust*
  the system — untrusted automation gets shut down at the first drawdown.

## Good system design: three pitfalls
Design for objective, simple, transparent, idea-based systems. Avoid:
1. **Over-fitting** (narrative fallacy: seeing patterns where none exist;
   overconfidence → under-diversified portfolios; optimisers assume precise
   knowledge of returns → extreme all-eggs-in-one-basket outputs).
2. **Overtrading** (illusory superiority → trade too often, costs eat edge;
   also addictive).
3. **Over-betting** (leverage amplifies a one-in-ten 5% loss day into a 50%
   wipeout day; 10x leverage ≈ 200% annualised stdev → expect ~40% one-day
   losses every ~3 years per the normal distribution; real tails are worse:
   the Dow fell >4 sigma ~30 times in 1914–2014 vs ~once-a-century predicted).

## Key formulas
- Annualised stdev = daily stdev × √256 ≈ ×16; daily = annualised ÷ 16.
- Annualised mean return = daily × 256 (means scale with time, vol with √time).
- Normal distribution: ±1σ ≈ 68% of days, ±2σ ≈ 95%; 2.5% of days worse than −2σ.

## Notes
- Back-testing is invaluable but dangerous when misused (foreshadows ch3).
- Volatility is used as shorthand for stdev of returns throughout the book.
- The unifying theme: size positions so you can *withstand* expected losses —
  "you need to be comfortable with the losses you're likely to make."
